What is Big Data?

Volume, velocity, variety and veracity

What is Big Data?

Big data is data that contains greater variety, arriving in increasing volumes and with ever-higher velocity. These are known as the three Vs.

VolumeVelocityVariety
Terabytes, records, transactions, tables and files Batch, near real time, real time, streams Structured, semi-structured, unstructured, all of the above

These characteristics make data hard to handle

60%

of AI projects without "AI-ready" data will be abandoned through 2026, Gartner predicts.

63%

of organizations were not confident their data management practices were ready for AI (Gartner survey, 2024).

The other big barrier is people. A shortage of skilled data and AI professionals remains one of the most frequently cited obstacles to data and AI success.

1.2 Velocity

How can we analyze and react to data generated at such rapid speeds?

Velocity refers to the speed at which data is created or updated, and how quickly it must be processed to be useful.

  • The world now creates hundreds of millions of terabytes per day (roughly 0.4–0.5 ZB). In 2023 the quoted figure was "more than 3 trillion GB per day": two orders of magnitude less.
  • "90% of the world's data was created in the last two years" is a rule of thumb for exponential growth, not a precise measurement.
  • IDC expected about a quarter of all data to be real-time by 2025, driven by IoT, sensors, mobile apps and online services.

1.3 Variety

How do we capture and analyze different types of data together?

TypeDescriptionExamples

Variety also means many different sources: sensors, phone calls, documents, video uploads, clickstreams, social media, and increasingly embeddings (vector representations used by AI systems).

1.4 Veracity

How do we know if data is accurate and trustworthy?

Veracity refers to the quality and trustworthiness of data. Because data comes from so many sources, it is hard to link, match, cleanse and transform consistently.

In the age of AI, veracity matters more: models amplify whatever is in their data, including errors and bias. That is why data quality, observability, lineage and governance are core parts of every data platform.

More data beats clever algorithms, but better data beats more data.Attributed to Peter Norvig

Why Big Data?

IT evolution, BI evolution and data value

2.1 IT evolution

Single-server relational databases hit limits

  • Scaling up is limited and very expensive
  • Designed mainly for structured data
  • Proprietary systems can lead to vendor lock-in

Meanwhile, year after year

  • Bigger, faster storage (SSD, NVMe) at a lower cost per TB
  • More CPU cores, plus GPUs and AI accelerators
  • More memory and much higher network bandwidth
  • Cloud: pay as you go, no large upfront investment
2026 note: distributed SQL engines and cloud warehouses have blurred the line between "relational" and "big data". Many big data systems today speak SQL.

2.2 From business intelligence to advanced analytics

Every company needs new analytical capabilities to stay competitive:

  • Handling any kind of data
  • Analyzing data and acting on it in real time
  • Scaling analytics as the business grows
  • Predicting customer behavior
  • Embedding AI into products and processes

Business intelligence (BI)

Business intelligence is an umbrella term that includes the applications, infrastructure and tools, and best practices that enable access to and analysis of information to improve and optimize decisions and performance.

Gartner

BI looks at the past. What happened? Description through reporting. Why did it happen? Diagnosis through interactive dashboards.

Advanced analytics (AA)

The autonomous or semi-autonomous examination of data or content using sophisticated techniques and tools, typically beyond those of traditional BI, to discover deeper insights, make predictions, or generate recommendations.

Gartner

AA looks at the future. What will happen? Prediction with ML models. What should we do? Prescription with recommenders and optimization.

Techniques

  • Data and text mining
  • Machine learning and forecasting
  • Semantic and sentiment analysis
  • Network and cluster analysis
  • Multivariate statistics
  • Deep neural networks
  • Generative AI (large language models)

The analytics value pyramid

PrescriptiveWhat should we do?
PredictiveWhat will happen?
DiagnosticWhy did it happen?
DescriptiveWhat happened?

Moving up the pyramid shifts the focus from hindsight to insight to foresight.

Each level adds business value and difficulty, and needs more (and better) data. Tools range from reports and KPIs at the base to ML, recommenders and AI agents at the top.

The most common use case: the data lake

A data lake is a centralized repository for all your structured and unstructured data at any scale. Data is stored as-is, without structuring it first, so all company data is available for analysis.

2026 update: poorly governed lakes became "data swamps". Most organizations now build a lakehouse: a data lake plus open table formats (Iceberg, Delta Lake) that add transactions, schemas and governance.

Benefits

  • Democratizes access to data
  • Supports all data formats
  • Schema flexibility (schema-on-read)
  • Enables advanced analytics and ML
  • Scales cheaply on object storage
  • A single, curated source of truth

The other common use case: customer 360

A 360° customer view is a holistic customer profile that captures every relevant piece of information about a customer: a single picture across sales, service, marketing and every other line of business.

These insights let companies deliver personalized, engaging experiences and reach their performance objectives. Today they also feed real-time personalization engines and AI assistants.

Big Data inception

The story of Hadoop

From a search engine to Hadoop

  1. 2002

    Doug Cutting and Mike Cafarella start Nutch, an open-source search engine. It won't scale to the whole web.

  2. 2003

    Google publishes the Google File System (GFS) paper.

  3. 2004

    Google publishes MapReduce. Cutting and Cafarella see the solution to their problem.

  4. 2005

    Nutch implements its own MapReduce and the Nutch Distributed File System.

  5. 2006

    The work becomes Hadoop; NDFS becomes HDFS. Cutting joins Yahoo!, which scales it up.

  6. 2008

    Hadoop becomes a top-level Apache project.

For the first time, free software could store and process huge amounts of data on clusters of commodity machines.

HDFS: Hadoop Distributed File System

A distributed, scalable file system that stores data in files and organizes them in directories.

Hadoop MapReduce

Data is spread across many machines, so we need distributed applications that run on many computers in parallel. MapReduce provides the libraries to write them (called jobs).

  • Map: each machine processes its local chunk and emits key–value pairs
  • Shuffle: pairs with the same key are grouped
  • Reduce: each group is combined into a final result

Example: word count

Input    Rat Mat Cat | Mat Mat Cat | Rat Mat Rat

Map      (Rat,1)(Mat,1)(Cat,1)
         (Mat,1)(Mat,1)(Cat,1)
         (Rat,1)(Mat,1)(Rat,1)

Shuffle  Rat → [1,1,1]
         Mat → [1,1,1,1]
         Cat → [1,1]

Reduce   Rat 3   Mat 4   Cat 2

Hadoop today

Hadoop is still maintained (release 3.5.0 is current), but it is now considered a legacy architecture.

Its ideas live on in every modern platform: distributed storage, moving computation to the data, and parallel processing. Understanding Hadoop is understanding why today's tools look the way they do.

The Big Data ecosystem

Ingestion, processing and serving

Why an ecosystem emerged

From 2008, big data technology was freely available as open source. Facebook, LinkedIn, Twitter, Netflix and others open-sourced their own tools, and the community built many projects around Hadoop.

Hadoop alone provides only storage and batch processing, so it addresses mainly volume and variety. The ecosystem fills three gaps:

Data ingestion
Data processing
Data serving

Data ingestion

Capturing, collecting and moving data into the storage layer in a scalable, reliable way. Hadoop has no full-fledged ingestion component.

Legacy

  • Apache Flume (logs)
  • Apache Sqoop (relational databases)

Sqoop has been retired to the Apache Attic, and Flume's last release dates from 2022.

Today

  • Apache Kafka: event streaming, the de facto standard
  • Apache NiFi
  • Change data capture (e.g. Debezium)
  • Managed cloud ingestion services

Data processing

How data is computed, transformed and analyzed to extract its value. Hadoop's only built-in engine is MapReduce.

MapReduce limitations

  • Hard to program: not every problem fits map and reduce
  • Mainly Java and JVM languages
  • Batch only, no streaming
  • Writes intermediate results to disk, which is slow

What we need now

  • Streaming processing
  • In-memory processing
  • SQL processing
  • Graph processing
  • Machine learning

Data serving

How processed data is delivered to end users and applications. Hadoop alone has no way to query data interactively.

When the results are big data too, they are served from:

  • NoSQL datastores: Cassandra, HBase, MongoDB (covered next term)
  • Search engines: Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Solr
  • Cloud warehouses and lakehouses for analytics
  • Vector databases and vector search for AI applications

Storage, compute and the lakehouse

Storage and compute separated

Hadoop kept data and computation on the same machines. Cloud platforms separate them: data sits cheaply in object storage, and compute engines scale up and down on demand.

Open table formats

Apache Iceberg, Delta Lake and Apache Hudi add database features (ACID transactions, schema evolution, time travel) on top of files in a data lake.

This lakehouse lets Spark, Flink, Trino and warehouses share one copy of the data, reducing vendor lock-in.

Streaming and data mesh

Streaming as a first-class citizen

Real-time pipelines are now mainstream. Apache Kafka 4.x no longer depends on ZooKeeper, and Flink and Spark Structured Streaming let us act on data within seconds.

Data mesh and data products

Decentralized ownership: domain teams publish their data as well-documented data products, supported by a self-service platform and federated governance.

Big data meets AI

Big data platforms are now the foundation of AI.

  • Large language models are trained on massive datasets
  • Companies ground AI assistants in their own data (retrieval-augmented generation) using embeddings and vector search
  • AI-ready data (high-quality, governed, well-documented) is now one of the main factors that decides whether AI projects succeed

Summary

Key takeaways and further reading

Key takeaways

  • Big data is a consequence of the evolution of data, technology and business intelligence, characterized by the 5 Vs.
  • It is a must-have capability because it enables advanced analytics and AI.
  • Use cases span every industry; data lakes/lakehouses and customer analytics are the most common.
  • Big data started with Hadoop, built on Google's GFS and MapReduce ideas, providing storage (HDFS) and processing (MapReduce).
  • The ecosystem keeps evolving: Spark replaced MapReduce, Kafka became the streaming standard, and cloud services replaced many clusters.
  • Today's trends: cloud-native lakehouses with open table formats, real-time streaming, and data platforms built to feed AI.
